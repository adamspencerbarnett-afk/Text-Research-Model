How our model works, step by step, with the real data

The model we are developing: a deterministic compiler that turns text into codes and back, two kinds of memory that are filled by adding text rather than by training, a small network that composes an answer, and a trust head that decides, code by code, which of them to believe. Every example below is taken from the actual components and today's runs, not made up.

The whole loop in one picture

Two of the four sources (memory and tables) have no learned parameters at all: they are filled by adding text and can be inspected, corrected or deleted line by line. The network and the copy head are learned. The trust head is learned too, and small: it is one linear layer that reads the network's state and each source's confidence.

Step 1. The compiler: text in, codes out, and back exactly

The compiler scans the text into units (a word with its leading space, a punctuation mark, a newline) and looks each unit up in one fixed dictionary of 8,192 entries fitted once on the training text. Rare words are spelled in pieces, then in raw bytes, so every input comes back byte for byte. There is nothing learned here, and the same compiler is used for the training set, for the user's question and for the model's answer.

Notice that ␣to is 284 and ␣the is 265 in both sentences: a word always gets the same code wherever it appears. That stability is what makes the memories possible. What we learned in the first phase: this step alone does not make the model smarter (the codes carry exactly the information of the text), but it makes replies 2.5× faster than byte models and gives the memories stable keys.

Step 2. The exchange memory (M2): find what we already know

Every question-and-answer pair the model has seen is stored under a key the compiler makes from the question: its content words, lower-cased, stop-words removed, order ignored. A new question is turned into a key the same way and looked up. An exact key returns the stored answer as it is; otherwise the nearest stored key by weighted word overlap is used. Adding a pair takes a millisecond and needs no training.

This is where the 99% exact figure comes from: ask a stored question again and the store returns its answer 99% of the time; add 511 new pairs after training and 99% of those are answered at once. It is lookup, not understanding. The paraphrase example shows its limit: "ecosystem" and "ecosystems" are different key words, so the best match is a different question.

Step 3. The count tables (M1): what usually comes next

From the training codes we count, for every one-code and two-code context, which codes followed it and how often, keeping the 64 most frequent. Looking a context up is a binary search; the table for a 250 MB corpus is rebuilt in seconds and lives on the GPU. In training, the current occurrence is removed from the count first (leave-one-out), otherwise the model learns to trust the tables blindly.

What the tables are good for: fluent, in-domain continuation and knowledge that can be added by reading (one Austen novel added to the counts improved prediction of another Austen novel with no training). What they are not good for: the question. After "Assistant:" the last two codes are the same for every question, so the tables alone give the same generic answer to everything. That was the first talking-bot failure, and it is why steps 2 and 5 exist.

Step 4. The network core (M3): read the evidence and the question

The network is a small Transformer. Its window is laid out so that the question is never far from the answer: first the retrieved exchange from memory, then a separator, then the user's question, then the answer as it is generated. Today's Q&A runs use the research core, width 128, 4 layers, 0.79M parameters (1.87M learned in total with embeddings and heads). The scale run trains cores of 4.7M and 19M.

The window the network sees (codes, shown as text)

             Assistant: Biodiversity is important to ecosystems because it helps to maintain healthy habitats …
[separator]  ⏎⏎
[question]   User: Why is biodiversity important to ecosystems?
             Assistant: ▌ the answer is produced here, one code at a time

Step 5. The copy head: carry the retrieved answer into the reply

This is the part added today. At each position the network's state attends over the states of the retrieved answer, and the attention says which code of the retrieved text follows the one I am at. Copying a passage is then a walk along it. It enters the trust head as a fourth source, with its own confidence (how peaked the attention is). Without it, the 0.8M network read the memory for scoring but could not act on it when generating: every question got the same generic reply.

Step 6. The trust head: who to believe, for this one code

For every code to be produced, the four sources each propose a probability distribution over the 8,192 codes. The trust head reads the network's state plus three numbers per table (how often the context was seen, how dominant its top follower is, how spread its followers are) and two per copy head (top weight, spread), and outputs four mixing weights that sum to one. The next code is sampled from the mixture. The head is one linear layer; it learns, for example, to trust the tables on function words and the copy head when the retrieved answer matches the question.

Step 7. Back to text

The produced codes are decoded by the same compiler, so the reply is exactly what the codes say, and the reply stops at the first code sequence that spells a new "User:" turn.

What the numbers mean

"The copy head took us from 0.13 to 0.48"

That number is word F1, not a share of questions understood. For each question we compare the words in the model's reply with the words in the reference answer. Precision is the share of the reply's words that are in the reference; recall is the share of the reference's words that the reply contains; F1 is their harmonic mean, between 0 and 1. The reported figure is the average over the questions in the group.

Worked example

reference: biodiversity is important to ecosystems because it helps to maintain healthy habitats   (12 words)
reply:     biodiversity is important to allow them to maintain healthy habitats                     (10 words)
in common: biodiversity, is, important, to, to, maintain, healthy, habitats = 8
precision 8/10 = 0.80   recall 8/12 = 0.67   F1 = 0.73

So 0.48 means that, on questions whose answer is in memory, the reply shares about half its words with the reference on average. Exact matches (the reply equal to the reference, every character) were 3% without the retrieval bias and 12% with it. 0.13 is what a fluent reply on a vaguely related topic scores by accident, which is why the no-memory group sits there. Neither number says anything about understanding; the test that will is answer accuracy on questions with a checkable answer, which is what GSM8K and the other sets fetched today are for.

What is not built yet

The check loop (verify a provisional answer against memory and an exact calculator, then refine, restart, or say "I don't know"), the number channel (numbers as values rather than digit codes), and training the Q&A design on the larger cores. These are the reasoning pieces, and they are next.

Bits per byte measures how well the model predicts text; it is the honest comparison for "lighter and faster at equal quality" and says nothing about intelligence. Answer accuracy on checkable questions is the measure we use for that.
