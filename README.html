What the research accomplished

A small model learned to turn language into checkable reasoning.

The central discovery is simple: first teach the system to write down who did what, to whom, and by how much. Once that meaning is correct, a very small reasoning system can apply it with high precision.

On the current routine development check, every internal program and both answer paths were correct across all three observed runs. Two of those runs also passed every harder development screen.

What worked—and why it matters.

These are the clearest demonstrations that explicit meaning and task-matched reasoning can let compact systems perform very accurately inside a defined problem.

Why this is significant

Teach meaning before calculation.

Many language models try to learn reading, knowledge, and reasoning inside one large network. This experiment deliberately separates reading from calculation so each part can be tested.

Plain-language guide

Think of the model as a careful clerk.

The clerk first converts a sentence into a small form with labeled boxes. A calculator then uses that form. If the final number is wrong, we can inspect the form and ask: Did the clerk misunderstand the sentence, or did the calculator apply it incorrectly?

Visible input

The system receives ordinary supported text.

Alice starts with 8 coins. Bob starts with 3 coins. Alice gives Bob 2 coins. How many coins does Bob have now?

No hidden event labels are supplied during normal inference. The parser must identify the action, people, amount, whether the action happened, and the question from the words it sees.

A major evaluation lesson

Why a correct final answer is not enough

Answer-only check

“Alice did not give Bob 2 coins.” If sender and receiver are accidentally reversed, balances still do not change. A balance answer can look correct.

Complete-program check

The internal record still exposes the reversed roles. V1 therefore checks every action and participant, not only the final number.

What these tests support

The compact structure in Barnett’s Model reduced the number of processed positions in the clearest short-workflow comparison. The later result kept lower latency with more parameters, so parameter count alone did not explain the timing. Architecture and the shape of the representation were plausible contributors.

What they do not prove

  • They do not measure the current 919K-parameter V1 parser.
  • They do not establish faster end-to-end answers on unrestricted text.
  • Phase 17 excluded preprocessing; Phase 14 preprocessing coverage is not fully documented.
  • The retained record does not preserve hardware, batch size, warm-up, checkpoint-file size, or peak-memory details.
  • A Phase 15 risk-prior shift slightly favored the subword control, so the compact model did not win every condition.

Where the research started

One compact-model idea became a study of meaning.

The starting question was whether a compact representation could reduce training needs on a local computer. Repeated tests separated storage size, model inputs, language understanding, and reasoning into different problems.

Historical journey

Original question

Can a small local model become more accurate by learning a compact form of meaning?

The goal was less training text, less computation, faster answers, and stronger reasoning.

What the experiments separated

  1. Compression
    How many bytes are stored.
  2. Representation
    What distinctions the model sees.
  3. Semantic compilation
    How language becomes explicit actions and relations.

The highest scoped precision repeatedly followed the third idea: identify the meaning correctly, then route it to the right operation.

9 milestones
  1. 01
    V6 · Compact arithmeticReported

    Represent sentences as changes to a state.

    Question

    Can a small model track transfers and balances?

    Result

    95.50% reported accuracy with 88,430 parameters and 19 compact positions.

    What changed next

    Test whether the same core could survive new names, items, and compositions.

    Why this result is qualified
    The reported test split was reused for checkpoint selection, so this is development-era evidence rather than an untouched final estimate.
  2. 02
    Phase 7 · Normalization and routingReported

    Remove surface variation before reasoning.

    Question

    Can one core handle new names and more than one item?

    Result

    95.24% across new names/items and 94.62% cross-item composition on 5,000 examples each.

    What changed next

    The front end became a compiler and router around reusable skills.

    What this did not prove
    The frozen core did not learn arbitrary language invariance itself. Deterministic preprocessing removed variation and exposed the variables it needed.
  3. 03
    Phases 8–10 · Orchestration and relationsReported

    Turn larger requests into explicit specialist operations.

    Question

    Can small skills be extended and combined?

    Result

    99.34–99.64% on four orchestration tasks; 100% on a graph benchmark; 94.74% on 5,000 cross-domain queries.

    What changed next

    Move from isolated skills toward dynamic state and composed programs.

    Why architecture mattered
    The successful graph model encoded message passing directly. Its generic baseline used a different architecture, so the result supports task-matched structure rather than a representation-only advantage.
  4. 04
    Phases 11–12 · Programs and scalingMixed evidence

    Composition worked; compressed long-state memory did not.

    Question

    Can the system handle references, dynamic graphs, and larger worlds?

    Result

    97.68% dynamic graphs, 93.64% event references, and 98.02% composed programs—but large-world streaming fell to 19.3%, 14.3%, and 13.6%.

    What changed next

    Separate “accepts longer input” from “retains the information needed to reason.”

  5. 05
    Phase 13 · The decisive parsing testTurning point

    The real bottleneck was understanding the sentence.

    Question

    Can the program be learned reliably from text?

    Result

    Learned parser: 36.52% exact programs and 51.28% answers. Controlled parser: 100% programs and 99.13% answers.

    What changed next

    Focus the architecture on text-to-structure learning and role binding.

    Why this changed the direction
    The reasoning system worked when it received a correct program. The hard problem was reliably discovering that program from unfamiliar language. The controlled parser covered only its designed grammar, so open-ended understanding remained unsolved.
  6. 06
    Phases 14–17 · Learned workflow controlsStronger controls

    Compact inputs helped Barnett’s Model in some synthetic conditions, not all.

    Question

    Would compact structure help when no exact arithmetic solution was available?

    Result

    Phase 14 reported ~0.250 ms for the 72,835-parameter Barnett’s Model versus ~0.666 ms for the 78,083-parameter BPE/subword control. Phase 17 reported 0.647 ms versus 2.152 ms, with 63.85% versus 62.02% balanced accuracy.

    What changed next

    Demand matched raw inputs, balanced metrics, and complete-cost comparisons.

    Why the headline needed care
    The clearest short-workflow test used 21 compact positions versus 193 subword positions. In the stronger long-context test, Barnett’s Model had about 17.3% more parameters. The timings were model latency rather than complete text-to-answer latency, and some other comparisons used unequal inputs. These results support further efficiency testing, not a universal advantage.
  7. 07
    Phases 18–30 · Long-stream stressUseful failure

    Longer execution did not mean better reasoning.

    Question

    Can fixed-size memory preserve decisions across hundreds of events?

    Result

    The model executed 640-event inputs, while balanced accuracy declined to roughly 42–47%. Later adapters and planners remained weak.

    What changed next

    Preserve source information, use retrieval, and measure quality at each length.

  8. 08
    Semantic V1 · Learned meaningCurrent

    Learn visible text into typed, executable events.

    Question

    Can a learned parser keep the benefits of explicit structure?

    Result

    100% routine programs and answers in all three observed training runs; every hard gate passed in two.

    What changed next

    Concentrate on unfamiliar word order and stable sender/receiver binding.

  9. 09
    Next version · Bounded improvementProposal

    Keep knowledge updates, skill learning, and active context separate.

    Question

    How can the system improve without interrupting a user or growing forever?

    Design

    Versioned memory, background candidates, bounded replay, retention gates, and rollback.

    What must happen first

    Stabilize interpretation, then test continual updates as a separate architecture.

Where research is needed next

Make the reading reliable before making the system larger.

The experiments have narrowed a broad ambition into a practical sequence. The immediate goal is robust interpretation; continuous improvement and broader knowledge come afterward.

Open work

Stabilize who did what to whom

Repair sender/receiver binding under unfamiliar word order, inactive events, and several simultaneous changes.

Repeat success across fresh starts

A recipe should pass every declared development seed, then unused confirmation seeds, rather than relying on one favorable model.

Protect the final evaluation

Use the 4,960 reserved worlds only after one fixed recipe qualifies. This makes the eventual result meaningful.

Expand language in measured stages

Add grammar, entities, operations, vocabulary, and out-of-domain detection separately so each gain and failure has a cause.

Test useful long memory

Measure retrieval and verified structured scans. Merely accepting a million tokens does not prove the system can use them accurately.

Add bounded continuous improvement

Train separate background candidates with verified examples and replay; publish only after new-skill, retention, resource, and rollback gates pass.

The architectural destination

Fast knowledge updates, careful skill updates, uninterrupted users.

New facts can enter a scoped memory without changing model weights. Repeated verified language failures can train a candidate in the background. Active conversations continue with one immutable version.

What this research has established

  • Explicit meaning can make a small specialist very precise.
  • Correct programs matter more than answers alone.
  • Architecture must match the required reasoning.
  • More width or more accepted input is not automatically better.
  • Robust text-to-structure learning is now the central bottleneck.

Possible directions from here

The research can follow several paths.

These routes are compatible, but they answer different questions. Each needs its own evidence rather than borrowing the high accuracy or speed of an earlier controlled task.

Decision map
Immediate foundation

1. Stabilize semantic parsing

Why: unfamiliar word order and sender/receiver binding are the current measured bottlenecks. Every later system depends on reading the request correctly.

First proof needed: one fixed recipe passes every development and unused confirmation seed on complete programs, not only answers.

Measurement

2. Compare Barnett’s Model with BPE fairly

Why: the historical speed results are promising, but they do not reveal how much came from representation, preprocessing, architecture, or the benchmark itself.

First proof needed: identical raw text, data, hardware, batch size, parameter budgets, and timing rules, including preprocessing and full text-to-answer latency.

Reliable reasoning

3. Combine learned language with exact operations

Why: a learned front end can accept more varied language while exact executors keep supported calculations and graph operations inspectable.

First proof needed: unseen paraphrases and adversarial role changes measured at both complete-program and answer levels.

Broader capability

4. Build a modular specialist system

Why: new skills can be added as tested modules and composed through explicit programs instead of relearning every ability inside one network.

First proof needed: a router composes several independently trained specialists while preserving accuracy and reporting the total parser, routing, and execution cost.

Knowledge and context

5. Add retrieval-backed long memory

Why: a large searchable library can provide long-term knowledge without forcing every stored token through neural attention on every request.

First proof needed: retrieval completeness, source-grounded answers, and useful reasoning quality as the document collection grows.

Self-improvement

6. Add bounded continual improvement

Why: verified failures could improve a fixed-capacity candidate in the background without changing the model used by active conversations.

First proof needed: new-skill gains survive retention, resource, concurrency, publication, and rollback gates under device-specific limits.

Local deployment

7. Optimize for small computers

Why: quantization, distillation, caching, and shorter representations may support private, offline, inexpensive use.

First proof needed: end-to-end latency, memory, storage, and accuracy comparisons on declared local hardware—not model-only timing.

Long-term scale

8. Train a broader language and knowledge model

Why: open conversation and broad knowledge require far more varied real-world data and capacity than the present controlled specialist contains.

First proof needed: a measured scaling curve and external evaluations showing that added data and parameters improve transferable language and reasoning enough to justify their cost.

A practical order

First stabilize the parser and run a fully matched comparison between Barnett’s Model and BPE. That establishes whether the central interface is reliable and whether the efficiency advantage survives fair end-to-end measurement. Modular skills, retrieval, continual improvement, and local optimization can then build on a stable contract. Training a broad general model remains a separate, much larger research program.
